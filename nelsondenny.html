What the Nelson-Denny is

The Nelson-Denny Reading Test (NDRT) measures three things: your vocabulary, your reading comprehension, and your reading rate. Police and fire departments, colleges, and training programs use it to check that you read at the level the job or course demands. Every answer is multiple choice with five options, and there's no penalty for guessing — a blank scores the same as a wrong answer, so never leave one empty.

The three sections
① Vocabulary80 items · 15 min

You're shown a word and five choices. Pick the one closest in meaning. That's roughly 11 seconds per item — trust your first read and keep moving.

② Comprehension38 items · 20 min

Seven reading passages (science, social studies, humanities) each followed by questions on the main idea, specific details, and inferences. That's about 2–3 minutes per passage including its questions.

③ Reading Ratefirst 1 min

During the first minute of the comprehension section you mark how far you've read. That's converted to words per minute and an estimated grade level. It's a diagnostic — read at your normal careful pace, don't race.

How it's scored

Your raw score (number correct) is converted into a grade equivalent — e.g. 12.5 means you read like a 12th-grader in the 5th month — and a percentile comparing you to others. Departments set their own cutoff, usually a grade level. Because guessing isn't penalized, answer every single question, even if you have to bubble blind in the last 30 seconds.

Vocabulary strategy
  • Break the word apart. Prefixes, roots and suffixes carry meaning — bene- = good, -cede = go/yield, -ous = full of.
  • Answer before you look. Guess the meaning in your head, then find the choice that matches — harder to be fooled by look-alikes.
  • Eliminate. Two choices are often obvious opposites planted as traps. Cross out what's clearly wrong and choose from what's left.
  • Feel its charge. If a word sounds positive or negative, drop choices with the opposite tone.
  • Don't stall. Circle a best guess, mark it, move on. One hard word isn't worth three easy ones.
Comprehension strategy
  • Read the questions first. Skim what you'll be asked so you read the passage with a purpose.
  • Find the main idea. The first and last sentences of a passage (and of each paragraph) usually carry it.
  • Detail questions live in the text. Go back and locate the exact line — don't answer from memory.
  • Inference ≠ invention. The answer must be supported by the passage, not by outside knowledge or a big leap.
  • Watch absolutes. Choices with always, never, all, none are often wrong — real passages hedge.
  • Budget time. ~2.8 min a passage. If a question is eating the clock, mark your best guess and come back.

Test-day checklist
  • Sleep and eat — reading fatigue is real over 35 minutes of focus.
  • It's timed and tight. Pace by the clock, don't perfect one item.
  • Bubble something for every question before time is called.
  • If you finish a section early, recheck flagged items only.
  • Confirm your department's current passing grade level before test day.
